# Airport Ops — demo script (scripted scenario, seed = 42)

This is the walkthrough script for showing the simulation to a mentor or
non-technical reviewer. It says exactly what should happen, and when, so
you can narrate *ahead* of the system ("watch — in a few seconds a
conflict will show up here") instead of reacting to it after the fact.

This was generated by actually running the real simulation code
tick-by-tick against `seed=42`, not written from guesswork. Every tick
number, timestamp, and flight ID below is real.

## Read this before you present it

Two different things are locked in by `seed=42`, and one thing isn't:

- **Locked (100% reproducible every time):** which flights exist, when
  each one arrives, how long each turnaround takes, the gate/crew
  inventory, when each disruption fires, and which flights it affects.
  This means *whether a conflict happens, and roughly when,* is
  guaranteed — it's simple math (more flights wanting gates than gates
  available), not a judgment call.
- **Not locked:** *which* flight the AI actually picks as the winner of a
  conflict, and the wording of its reasoning. That comes from a live call
  to the language model, so it can reason differently from one run to the
  next. If your mentor watches a different flight win a gate than the one
  named below, that's expected — it's not a bug, and it's actually a good
  thing to point out live ("see, it's making a real judgment call here,
  not replaying a script").

So narrate the **structure** ("a conflict is about to happen right here,
because three planes are about to want two gates") and treat the specific
outcome as a bonus, not a promise.

## Setup, before you press play

- 18 flights, scheduled between 08:10 and 11:29
- 6 gates: 4 that only fit smaller ("narrow-body") planes, 2 that can
  take either size. This is deliberate: there are 3 large ("wide-body")
  planes landing within a couple of minutes of each other, and only 2
  gates that can take them. That's not an accident — it's the whole
  point of the demo.
- 12 ground crew (4 each for baggage, pushback, cleaning)
- 3 pre-written disruptions: one big weather event, one small crew
  shortage, one tiny single-flight technical issue

Recommended: switch to manual step mode and click through it slowly for
a live audience, rather than letting it run on the timer. Each click
advances 5 minutes of simulated time.

## The walkthrough

### T+0 to T+10 — the setup (nothing to point out yet)
Five flights take off toward the airport almost simultaneously: three
large planes (6E854, QP529, QP532) and two small ones (AI125, 6E617).
This is the "bottleneck" — say out loud that these five are all going to
want somewhere to land in the next few minutes, and there's only room for
some of them.

### T+15 — first landings, first conflict (tick 3)
6E854 and QP529 (both large planes) land at the same time and both want
the same gate. **This is the first thing to point at.** The system
notices the clash and picks one — in the verified run, 6E854 won and
QP529 had to wait. Say: "two planes just landed and both wanted the same
spot — watch it get sorted out automatically."

### T+20 — a second and third conflict (tick 4)
Three more flights land at once (AI125, QP532, 6E617) and this time there
are two separate gate clashes. Also: the first crew assignment happens
for 6E854 (baggage, pushback, and cleaning staff all get assigned
together).

### T+20 to T+105 — QP532 is stuck, and that's the demo
QP532 landed at T+20 but **does not get a gate until T+105** — roughly
85 minutes (17 clicks) later. Every tick in between, the system logs "no
compatible gate available." This is the single best thing to show a
mentor: point at it early ("see this plane? it landed but there's nowhere
for it yet — watch, it'll just sit there for a while") and then circle
back later to show it finally getting resolved. It's a real, visible
consequence of there only being 2 large-plane gates.

### T+35 — the weather disruption fires (tick 7)
Right on schedule, a severe weather disruption triggers, covering all
five of the original bottleneck flights. From here on, every tick shows
the disruption as "unresolved, monitoring" — this is the system watching
and re-evaluating it every single time it ticks, not ignoring it.

### T+85–90 — a second disruption overlaps (tick ~17–18)
A crew-shortage disruption fires on top of the still-open weather one,
affecting two more flights. This is a good moment to say: "now there are
two separate problems happening at once — watch whether it handles them
both."

### T+105 — the big payoff moment (tick 21)
In a single tick: QP532 *finally* gets its gate, a second stuck plane
(SG691) gets a gate too, **and both disruptions get marked resolved in
the same tick** — because the thing they were both waiting on (every
affected flight having a gate) just became true. This is the moment to
build the whole demo around: "everything that was stuck for the last
90 minutes just got resolved, automatically, the instant it was actually
possible."

### T+185–195 — the small disruption, for contrast (tick 37–39)
A tiny disruption affecting just one flight (AI665) fires, sits for two
ticks, then resolves as soon as that one flight gets its gate. Good
contrast to point out: "that big weather problem took 90 minutes to
clear because it depended on five planes and two scarce gates — this one
clears in 10 minutes because it only depended on one plane and there was
a free gate right away." Same mechanism, different outcome, because the
underlying situation was genuinely different — not because the system
treated them differently.

### T+220 — end state (tick 44, a good place to stop)
15 of 18 flights have fully departed, 2 are still at their gate finishing
up, 1 is still on the ground waiting its turn. Every disruption that was
going to resolve by this point has resolved. Good closing line: "18
flights, 3 unplanned problems, zero manual intervention."

## One more finding while building this

Separately from the script above: at the very end of this run,
`open_conflicts` still had 34 entries marked unresolved, even though the
flights involved clearly did get sorted out later. That's a real,
separate issue — a `Conflict` record made when a flight briefly had zero
available crew never gets cleaned up once that flight succeeds on a
later attempt, so the count of "unresolved conflicts" grows over a long
run even when nothing is actually stuck anymore. Worth fixing at some
point (it would misrepresent system health on a long-running dashboard),
but it doesn't affect anything in the walkthrough above.
